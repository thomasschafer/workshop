# Learning in Workshop

## The home screen

Workshop opens on a home screen, whose Continue button goes straight to the most sensible next step, such as the puzzle you were last working on or the cards due for review. Below it are the tracks: Advent of Code, Coding, and tracks of short items from a pack. A pack's own track can keep to itself, so Continue never offers it and it has limits of its own, for something you only practise when you open it. Everything takes a few minutes at a time unless you turn on "I've got a while", which lets Continue offer long items too: design sessions and timed rounds, taking turns, though one you've left unfinished always comes first. It stays on while you're using the app, and turns itself off once you've been away for half an hour.

## Advent of Code

Pick a year and a day, and Workshop fetches the puzzle and your input. Choose which example is the sample, then write your solution in the editor, run it on the sample and on your input, and submit, with inline errors and types from your language server. Haskell, Python, TypeScript, Go and Rust are built in, and other languages can be added in your config. Alongside the code are notes, snippets, and a discuss tab: a coach that sees the puzzle and your code, and won't give the answer away unless you ask. Solutions can be committed to git after each star; [setup.md](setup.md#your-solutions) says how to run them from your own editor too.

To try it before logging in, or outside December, open the practice puzzle from the bottom of the puzzle list.

## Packs and practice

Besides Advent of Code, Workshop practises short items from a pack: review cards you mark yourself, mental maths with fresh numbers each time, and probability questions with exact answers. A pack is a directory of Markdown files, usually a git repo of your own; [packs.md](packs.md) describes the format. Until you configure one, Workshop uses its small sample pack.

Practice runs straight from one item to the next until there's nothing left in the track for now, showing how many you've done in this sitting and this week's minutes above each item. Each answer is saved as you give it, so you can stop at any moment. Items come back when you're due to forget them, scheduled with [FSRS](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-Algorithm). Continue puts due reviews first, up to a daily limit, and the home screen counts your practice towards a weekly target rather than a streak.

Short items work offline: once a phone has opened Workshop, it keeps what it needs to practise, and syncs your answers when it can reach the server again. As it keeps the app itself too, a phone carries on with the version it opened until it's reloaded: once a newer one is ready, a card at the top offers to reload, and coming back to the app after a while reloads it by itself, at home or on a puzzle with nothing under way.

## Lessons

A pack can include short lessons that teach a topic in a few minutes before its questions. In a track's practice flow, after any cards being followed up and due items and before new items, Workshop offers the track's next lesson: the first in the pack's order that you haven't done yet and whose prerequisites are done. It appears as a short card showing its title, what it covers and roughly how long it takes, with Start and Not now. Start opens the lesson; finishing it, or going back from it, returns to the flow, which carries on. Not now skips that lesson for the rest of your day. At most `daily_lessons` lessons are offered a day (1 by default; a track kept apart has its own count of the same size). Where Continue would go to a track's new items, it goes to that track's next lesson first, naming it as a lesson, and each track's line on the home screen mentions a lesson waiting alongside its due and new counts. All of a track's lessons are also listed in a Lessons section on its practice screen once there's nothing left to practise for now, each with how long it roughly takes, whether you've done it, and any lessons it asks you to do first (though any lesson can still be opened from the list).

Questions linked to a lesson wait until you've done that lesson, and until the next day after you first did it (by your own day, as daily limits count days), so recall isn't tested straight after reading. Counts of new items on the home screen leave out items still waiting for their lesson. An item you've already attempted carries on being scheduled as usual, whatever its lesson's state.

Before you answer a short item linked to a lesson, it offers "Teach me" alongside its other ways to answer. That counts as not knowing the item — the same as "I don't know" or rating a card Forgot — then opens the whole lesson if you haven't done it yet, or just the item's segment (with that segment's check) if you have and the item names one; finishing it returns to the next item in the flow. A whole lesson finished this way counts as done, while revisiting a single segment doesn't add a completion. A short item without a lesson offers "Teach me" too: it counts as not knowing the item, shows its answer and explanation as not knowing it does, and records a request for a lesson on that item, synced like attempts so it works offline. Requests waiting are listed under Packs, under the author, and the next author run is told of them first so it can write a lesson and link them to it; once an item names a lesson, the request is answered and "Teach me" opens that lesson instead. A request for an item that's gone from its pack is dropped. When a linked item is answered wrong or rated Forgot twice in a row, the answer screen offers "Revisit the lesson" beside Next, opening its segment (or the whole lesson without one) before returning to the flow.

A lesson moves a screen at a time: an opening saying what it covers, any unscored guesses (with "Not sure" and no feedback until the end, to focus attention on what follows), short segments with diagrams and quick checks that show their explanation as soon as you answer, an explain-in-your-own-words question, and a recap. A small progress bar tracks the segments and lets you go back to earlier ones. At the explain step you can type, dictate, or explain aloud: online, the grader marks your explanation against the lesson's explain answer and segments; offline, or whenever you choose, you can compare it with the explain answer yourself. Reaching the recap shows each guess alongside the right answer, "Read more" links to its sources, and records the lesson as done along with how long it took, counting towards the week's minutes like any other practice (capped as attempts are; a lesson can be done again, and each time is recorded). Where you are in a lesson, and its images, stay on the device, so you can do a lesson offline, leave part-way and carry on where you were, and sync completion once the server can be reached. Lessons aren't scheduled for review, and their explain step doesn't log mistakes or feed mastery.

If you already know what a lesson teaches, "I know this", beside Start on the flow's lesson card and on a lesson opened that you haven't done, checks that instead of teaching it. It asks the lesson's checks one at a time, without the segments before them, saying only whether each answer was right, then its explain question, typed or dictated and marked by the grader. Every check right and the grader's score at least 85% (what counts as Good when it marks other work) passes: the lesson counts as done, just as finishing it would, so its questions wait until the next day, and you see your explanation beside the explain answer, with "Read more" links to its sources, before going back to the flow or the list. If a check was missed it doesn't ask for an explanation, as that couldn't pass. Not passing shows the checks you missed, with their explanations, and the grader's feedback, then offers the lesson, starting at the first segment whose check you missed (or at the start if it was the explanation that fell short), or Not now, which skips it for the day as on the lesson card. Not passing doesn't count the lesson as done or log mistakes. As the explanation has to be marked, the check can't be passed offline, so offline it says so and offers the lesson instead. Where you are in a check is saved on the device as you go, as a lesson is. A lesson with fewer than two checks doesn't offer it.

## Coding

The Coding track has problems to solve in Python, Rust or Haskell, fully on a phone, and code to read in Python, Rust, Haskell or C++. A problem is a chain of short steps: understand it, by working out what an example prints; plan, typed or dictated; code, in Workshop's editor, with Helix's keybindings and Workshop's own keyboard if you like, and the language server; then submit it, to hidden tests and a comparison with a slow but sure solution on random inputs, which shows you a small input it fails on if it's wrong. Last, compare it with how it's meant to be solved, and say how it went. Each step is saved as you go, your code included, so you can stop at any moment and Continue picks up where you were, on any device. If the problem's language, or its language server, isn't installed, a problem in it offers to install it with mise, for the Coding track only. It also has work in a codebase someone else wrote, a small project of a few files to switch between: fixing a bug from its symptom, reviewing a change by commenting on its lines, or refactoring code so tests can be written for it, then extending it.

A tutor gives hints that don't give the problem away, through your discussion model. Problems come back for review as a short twist on them, posed by the tutor and marked by the grader, or solved again if they went badly; a problem you rate easy is retired, and the author writes harder ones in its skills. Code to read asks what it prints, what's wrong with it or what it costs, and works offline; running code and the tutor need the server. Answered right, code to read or a probability question can be retired as too easy, so it isn't learnt by heart, and the author writes harder variants of it.

`workshop pack check` checks every item in a pack: that it can be read, that a probability question's simulation agrees with its answer, that a mental maths template makes sensible questions, that a coding problem's solutions pass its tests and agree with its brute force on random inputs, and that code to read does what its answer says.


## Design sessions and timed rounds

A design prompt is worked through as a session in stages, from the requirements to the trade-offs. You find out the requirements and the scale by asking the coach, which answers from a brief you see only at the end, then it asks you follow-up questions about your design, and the grader marks the whole session, and what you asked, at the end. Each stage is saved as you write, so you can stop at any of them.

A timed round puts a coding problem, a probability question or a design prompt against a visible clock, with an examiner who answers your questions and asks follow-ups that extend or change the problem, as a live session would. When time's up, the grader marks your working and what was said. The clock only runs while the round is open: close the app and come back within half an hour, and it carries on where it was; leave it longer, and the round ends there and is marked on what you did, without counting as an attempt. A pack can offer styles for the examiner, such as how many hints it gives and how hard it probes, to choose from as a round starts.

Cards, coding plans and solutions, design notes and what you say to the coaches can all be dictated, and some can be explained aloud: the grader then marks how clearly you put it, as well as whether it's right.

A card answered in your own words can go deeper: the tutor asks a few follow-up questions about what you said, such as why it's so or what happens in an edge case, and the grader marks the whole exchange for how deeply you understand it. Each answer is saved as you give it, so you can close the app part-way and carry on.

## Mistakes, goals and the grader

Wrong answers, failed submissions and what the grader finds go into a mistake log, each tagged with a kind of mistake, such as an off-by-one or a misreading, which you can confirm or change; a pack can add kinds of its own. Practice, and Continue, lean towards the skills your recent mistakes are in. Each goal a pack defines has a page showing each of its skills' coverage beside its recall, such as "40% covered · recall 85%", against the goal's targets, how both have changed over the last few weeks, how long is left, and what's weakest. Coverage is how much of a skill has been taught and then recalled: the share of its topics covered, where its pack outlines it in topics that lessons teach, or otherwise the share of its items answered right. Recall is how well what's been taught is remembered now. A goal is reached once every skill's coverage and recall have both reached their targets, and the weakest skills are those lowest in either; [packs.md](packs.md#packtoml) has the details.

The grader, through your discussion provider's first model unless you give it one of its own under Settings, Models, or `[models.grader]` in your config, marks written and spoken work against a rubric: design sessions, timed rounds, a coding problem's plan and how its solution works, a card answered in your own words (with any follow-up questions on it), and a lesson's explain question. For everything except lessons, its score counts towards scheduling and mastery, and what it finds goes in the mistake log. It also checks cards you've flagged with "This is wrong", unflagging those that are right, and proposing a fix for those that aren't, which changes your pack once you approve it.

The author writes new items for a pack of your own, for what you're weakest in and the mistakes you make most: one model writes them, a model from another provider solves each blind and reviews it, and each must pass its check. Run it with `workshop author`, or Top up under Packs in the app, or have the server run it every so often, with `every` under `[author]`; [author.md](author.md) describes it. Questions you've found elsewhere can be added to a pack as drafts, under Packs or with `workshop pack import`, for the author to make into items.
